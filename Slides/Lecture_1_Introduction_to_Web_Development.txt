LECTURE 1: INTRODUCTION TO WEB DEVELOPMENT
For Absolute Beginners
Suggested duration: 90 minutes

MAIN OBJECTIVE
By the end of this lecture, students should understand what the Web is, how a website works at a basic level, the main areas of web development, and how to create and open a very simple HTML page.

============================================================
1. WHAT IS THE INTERNET? (10 MINUTES)
============================================================

Start by asking:
"When you open Google, YouTube, or Facebook, what actually happens?"

Explain these terms:

- Internet: A global network that connects computers and other devices.
1. Before the Internet existed

Imagine the world in the 1950s and 1960s.

    There were computers, but they were very large.

    Computers were expensive and mostly used by governments, universities, and research organizations.

    Computers worked independently.

    If one university wanted to share information with another university, it was difficult.

Scientists started thinking:

"What if we could connect computers in different places so they could communicate and share information?"

That was one of the big ideas behind the Internet.
2. Why was the Internet created?

In the 1960s, researchers in the United States began developing a project called ARPANET.

It was funded by an organization called ARPA (Advanced Research Projects Agency).

Their goals included:

    Connecting computers at different research institutions.

    Sharing computing resources.

    Allowing researchers to exchange information.

    Developing communication that could continue working even if parts of a network stopped working.

The important idea was to connect separate computers through a communication network.

In 1969, ARPANET connected its first computers at four research institutions:

    UCLA

    Stanford Research Institute

    University of California, Santa Barbara

    University of Utah

This was an important beginning of the technology that eventually became the Internet.

Step 1: A university creates its own network
Imagine University A has 100 computers.

Students, teachers, and researchers want to share information between these computers.

So the university creates a Local Area Network (LAN).

For example:
- Computer 1
- Computer 2
- Computer 3
- University server
- Network switches and cables
All these devices can communicate within the university's network.

University B does the same thing.

Now we have two independent networks.
- University A has its own network.
- University B has its own network.
But they cannot automatically communicate with each other just because both have networks.

We need to connect them.
Step 2: How did University A connect to University B?
In the early days, universities could use dedicated communication lines to connect their networks.
The basic idea:
1. University A has its own LAN.
2. University B has its own LAN.
3. They arrange a communication link between their networks.
4. Networking equipment at each end sends data through that link.
5. Computers on both university networks can exchange information.
But what was the communication link?

It could be a dedicated telephone-company circuit, also called a leased line.

A leased line is a communication connection rented from a telecommunications company.

For example:
- University A rents a connection.
- University B rents a connection.
- The telephone company provides the infrastructure between them.
Remember, these weren't ordinary telephone calls. The telephone infrastructure was being used to carry computer data.
Step 3: What if 100 universities wanted to connect?
Now imagine 100 universities.

Would every university need a separate direct connection to all the other 99 universities?

That would be expensive and difficult to manage.

Instead, researchers developed the idea of connecting university networks through larger networks.

This is where the concept of a backbone network becomes important.

Think of a backbone as a major highway.
- University LAN = Local roads.
- Regional network = Roads connecting nearby cities.
- Backbone network = Major national highway.
- Other backbone networks = Highways connecting different regions and countries.
A university could connect to a regional network, and that regional network could connect to a larger backbone.

Now universities didn't need individual direct connections to every other university.


Step 4: Enter NSFNET – A major turning point









NSFNET

National Science Foundation Network
1986


The United States National Science Foundation (NSF) wanted researchers and universities to communicate and access powerful supercomputers.

So it established NSFNET.

Here's what happened:
1. NSF supported the creation of a high-speed network.
2. It connected university-based supercomputer centers.
3. Regional academic networks connected to this larger network.
4. More universities gained access through those regional networks.
5. The network expanded over time.
The important historical point: NSFNET became a major backbone for the growing Internet.

The original NSFNET backbone became operational in 1986. By 1993, it connected more than two million computers. 
his is how the university networking story grew beyond the original four ARPANET institutions.

One important distinction: Not every university joined ARPANET directly. Other academic networks and NSFNET helped expand access to many more institutions.

Step 5: How did networks in different countries connect?
Now let's move beyond the United States.

Imagine:
- University A is in the USA.
- University B is in the UK.
- University C is in Japan.
- University D is in Australia.
Each country and organization could develop its own research networks.

But how could these networks communicate?

They needed two things:

1. Physical connectivity

A physical connection that could carry data between locations.

Examples:
- Fiber-optic cables.
- Undersea communication cables.
- Telecommunications infrastructure.
- Satellite links in some locations.

2. Common communication protocols

They needed common rules so that different networks could understand and exchange data.

This is where TCP/IP became important.

Remember:
- IP helps route packets toward their destinations.
- TCP provides reliable, ordered delivery when TCP is used.
Without compatible networking protocols, connecting physical cables alone would not make different networks communicate properly.

The Internet Society's history describes the development of TCP/IP specifically to interconnect different packet networks.

Step 6: How did ordinary people get Internet access?
Until this point, we've mainly discussed research institutions and universities.

But eventually, businesses and ordinary people wanted Internet access too.

This is where Internet Service Providers (ISPs) became important.

An ISP is a company that provides Internet connectivity to customers.

Examples include:
- PTCL
- Nayatel
- StormFiber
Imagine you subscribe to an Internet connection from PTCL.

Here's a simplified picture:

1. You subscribe to an ISP.
2. The ISP provides a connection to your home.
3. Your router connects your devices to that service.
4. Your ISP carries your data through its network.
5. Your ISP connects with other networks to reach destinations outside its own network.
For example, when you open YouTube, your ISP helps carry your request toward Google's network.

Your ISP doesn't need to own YouTube's servers or the entire Internet.

It just needs a way to exchange traffic with the other networks.
Step 7: How do different ISPs connect to each other?
This is an extremely important concept.

Imagine:
- ISP A serves your home.
- ISP B serves another person's home.
- ISP C operates a different network.
How can your computer communicate with someone using ISP B?

The ISPs connect their networks to each other.

There are two important concepts:

Peering: Two networks agree to exchange traffic directly.

Transit: One network pays another network to carry traffic to other networks that it cannot reach directly.

They can connect through dedicated links or Internet Exchange Points (IXPs).

An IXP is a physical location where different networks connect to exchange Internet traffic.

For example:

ISP A → IXP ← ISP B

Now traffic can move between their networks through that connection.

This is another reason why the Internet is called a network of networks.

Part 2: Who owns the Internet?
This is a really interesting question.

Imagine you open Google, then Facebook, then YouTube.

All these services communicate through the Internet.

So who owns the Internet that connects them?

Is it Google? Is it the US government? Is there one company with a giant computer controlling everything?
The answer: No single owner
The Internet is not owned by one person, company, or government.

It is a collection of independently operated networks that connect and communicate with each other.

1. Who owns the physical infrastructure?
Although nobody owns the entire Internet, different organizations own and operate different parts of its infrastructure.

For example:
Infrastructure	Who may own or operate it?
Fiber-optic cables	Telecom companies and infrastructure operators
Undersea cables	Telecommunications companies and consortia
Routers	ISPs, companies and institutions
Data centers	Cloud companies and other organizations
University networks	Universities
ISP networks	Internet service providers

Imagine PTCL owns a fiber-optic cable connecting two cities.

That cable belongs to PTCL or its infrastructure operator.

Another company may own a different cable connecting different locations.

They can connect their networks and exchange data.

So, different organizations own different pieces of the physical infrastructure.

Nobody needs to own every cable, router, server or data center to make the Internet work.
2. Is there one main computer controlling the Internet?
No.

There is no single central computer that stores or controls the entire Internet.

Let's use an example.

Imagine:
- Google has its own servers and networks.
- YouTube operates its services using Google's infrastructure.
- Facebook operates its own infrastructure.
- PTCL operates its network.
- Universities operate their own networks.
These organizations manage their own systems.

They communicate with other networks using common protocols and agreed arrangements.

If one company's network experiences an outage, other independent networks can continue operating.

Of course, the services and connections depending on the failed network may become unavailable.

This is where things become interesting.

Although nobody owns the entire Internet, different organizations coordinate particular technical functions and standards.

Here are some important organizations:

4. Can a government control the Internet?
Governments can influence and regulate Internet access within their countries.

For example, a government can:
- Regulate telecommunications companies.
- Establish rules for Internet service providers.
- Block certain websites or services under applicable laws.
- Regulate telecommunications infrastructure.
- Establish cybersecurity and data protection requirements.
However, controlling Internet services or infrastructure within a country is not the same as owning the entire worldwide Internet.

Every country has its own laws and regulatory systems.
5. What happens if one ISP shuts down?
Let's imagine Pakistan has three different ISPs:
- ISP A
- ISP B
- ISP C
If ISP A experiences a network outage:
- Customers using ISP A may lose Internet access.
- Customers using ISP B may continue using the Internet.
- Customers using ISP C may continue using the Internet.
This is because the Internet consists of many interconnected networks rather than one central network.

There can still be wider outages if the failed infrastructure is shared by several providers.

Imagine you are sitting at home in Gujranwala, Pakistan, using your laptop.

You open www.youtube.com.

What happens?

Your laptop connects to your Wi-Fi router.



Your router sends your traffic through your Internet connection.



Your ISP carries the traffic through its network.



Your ISP forwards the traffic through connections to other networks.



The request reaches Google's infrastructure.



Google's systems send the requested data back.



Your ISP carries the response back to your device.



Your browser displays YouTube.

This is a simplified example; the actual route can involve many networks, routers and data centers.

The important thing is that your ISP doesn't own Google, and Google doesn't own your ISP. They communicate through interconnected networks.

These seven concepts are the foundation of networking. Before teaching HTML, CSS and JavaScript, I recommend giving students a basic understanding of how computers communicate over the Internet.


Imagine you're sitting at home with your laptop. You open Chrome and type:

https://www.google.com

A lot of things happen behind the scenes:
1. Your laptop communicates with your router.
2. Your device uses an IP address to communicate over the network.
3. DNS helps find the IP address associated with the domain name.
4. Routers help forward data toward its destination.
5. HTTP or HTTPS defines how the browser and web server communicate.
All seven concepts fit into this one example.

IP stands for Internet Protocol.

An IP address is a logical address assigned to a network interface so that it can be identified and reached using the Internet Protocol.

Think of it like a home address.

Imagine you want to send a parcel to someone.

You need their address so the delivery service knows where to send it.

Similarly, when computers communicate over an IP network, they use IP addresses to identify the source and destination of data.


MAC stands for Media Access Control.

A MAC address is a link-layer address associated with a network interface.

Think of it this way:
- IP address: Used for communication across IP networks.
- MAC address: Used for communication on a local network link.

Where is it used?
Imagine your laptop and mobile are connected to the same Wi-Fi router.

When your laptop needs to send a frame to another device on the same local network, the network uses MAC addresses to deliver that frame over the local link.

A router is a networking device that connects different IP networks and forwards data between them.

Let's understand this with an example.

Imagine you have:
- A laptop at home.
- A mobile phone.
- A smart TV.
- A Wi-Fi router.
All three devices connect to your router.

Your router connects your home network to your ISP's network.
What does a router actually do?
Its main job is to forward data toward the correct destination.

Imagine your laptop wants to access Google's website.
1. Your laptop prepares data to send.
2. The data is sent toward your router.
3. The router checks the destination IP address.
4. It checks its routing table to decide where to forward the data.
5. It sends the data toward the next network.

What is the difference between a router and Wi-Fi?
These are not the same thing.
- Router: Forwards traffic between networks.
- Wi-Fi: A wireless technology that allows devices to connect to a network.

DNS stands for Domain Name System.

This is one of the most important concepts for Web Development.

Let's say you want to visit Google's website.

But computers communicate using IP addresses.

So how does your browser know where to send the request?

This is where DNS comes in.

DNS translates domain names into IP addresses.

Think of DNS as the contact list on your phone.

You save someone's name as "Ali".

When you want to call Ali, you don't need to memorize his phone number. You search for his name.

Similarly:
- Domain name = Contact name.
- IP address = Phone number.
- DNS = Contact lookup system.

Who provides DNS services?
Your ISP may provide a DNS resolver.

5. The big invention: TCP/IP

In the 1970s, two researchers, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, developed the foundations of TCP/IP.

Think of TCP/IP as a common language and set of rules that allows different networks to communicate.

    IP: Helps data find the correct destination.

    TCP: Helps ensure data arrives correctly and in the right order.

This was a breakthrough because separate networks could now communicate with each other.

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET officially switched to TCP/IP. This is considered a major milestone in the birth of the modern Internet.

2. HOW DOES A WEBSITE WORK? (15 MINUTES)
============================================================

Introduce these concepts:

- Client: The device or software that makes a request, such as a web browser.
- Server: A computer or program that receives requests and provides responses.
- Browser: Software used to access and display websites, such as Chrome or Firefox.
- Request: A message sent by a client asking for a resource or service.
- Response: The reply sent back by the server.
- HTTP: A protocol used for communication between web clients and servers.
- HTTPS: The secure version of HTTP, using encryption for the connection.
- Domain name: A human-readable website address, such as example.com.
- Hosting: A service that makes a website or application available on the Internet.

Simple example: Opening www.google.com

1. A user enters a website address in the browser.
2. The browser works out where to send the request.
3. The browser sends a request to the website's server.
4. The server sends a response.
5. The browser processes the response and displays the page.

Keep this explanation high-level. Detailed DNS and networking concepts can be taught later.

3. WHAT IS WEB DEVELOPMENT? (10 MINUTES)
============================================================

Web development is the work involved in creating and maintaining websites and web applications.

Introduce the three core frontend technologies:

HTML - STRUCTURE
- Defines the content and structure of a web page.
- Examples: headings, paragraphs, images, links, and buttons.

CSS - STYLING
- Controls how the page looks.
- Examples: colors, fonts, spacing, and layouts.

JavaScript - FUNCTIONALITY
- Adds behavior and interactivity to a page.
- Examples: responding to button clicks, validating forms, and updating content.

Simple house analogy:
- HTML is like the structure and rooms of a house.
- CSS is like the paint and decoration.
- JavaScript is like the electrical and interactive systems.

============================================================
4. TYPES OF WEB DEVELOPMENT (10 MINUTES)
============================================================

FRONTEND DEVELOPMENT
- Focuses on the part of a website users see and interact with.
- Common technologies in this course: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Vue.js.

BACKEND DEVELOPMENT
- Focuses on server-side logic and working with data.
- Common responsibilities: handling requests, applying application logic, authentication, and communicating with databases.
- Technologies in this course: Node.js, Express.js, and MongoDB.

FULL-STACK DEVELOPMENT
- Involves working with both frontend and backend parts of an application.

6. FIRST PRACTICAL: CREATE AN HTML PAGE (15 MINUTES)
============================================================

Open VS Code and create a file named:

index.html

Type this code:

<!DOCTYPE html>
<html>
<head>
    <title>My First Website</title>
</head>
<body>
    <h1>Hello World!</h1>
    <p>Welcome to Web Development.</p>
    <button>Click Me</button>
</body>
</html>

Explain each part:

- <!DOCTYPE html>
  Tells the browser that this document uses HTML5.

- <html>
  The root element that contains the HTML document.

- <head>
  Contains information about the document that is not displayed as normal page content.

- <title>
  Sets the title shown in the browser tab.

- <body>
  Contains the page content displayed in the browser.

- <h1>
  Creates a main heading.

- <p>
  Creates a paragraph.

- <button>
  Creates a button. It will not perform an action until functionality is added.

Practical steps:
1. Open VS Code.
2. Create a new folder for the first lesson.
3. Create index.html inside that folder.
4. Type the example code.
5. Save the file.
6. Open the file in a browser.
7. Change the heading and paragraph, save, and refresh the browser.

Important:
Do not teach all HTML tags in this lecture. The next lecture will cover HTML in more detail.
